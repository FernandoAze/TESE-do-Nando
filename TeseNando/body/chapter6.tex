\chapter{Conclusions and Future Work} \label{chap:conclusion}
\section{Achievements}

This thesis set out to address the lack of a reusable framework for composing score-aligned, multi-modal \ac{MIR} visualisations, and to assess whether such a framework could meaningfully support beat annotation and beat-tracking evaluation workflows. Revisited against the objectives and research questions set out in \autoref{chap:intro}, the work can be summarised as follows.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Objective 1} was met through the development of LayerIt, a Python, object-oriented framework in which score, audio, and algorithmic data are treated as independent, composable layers. \autoref{chap:DesignAndImplementation} showed that this ``layers within layers'' philosophy holds consistently at the visual, SVG, and code levels, giving the framework a coherent architecture rather than a single-purpose script.

    \item \textbf{Objective 2} was addressed in \autoref{chap:Validation}, where RQ1 was answered by reproducing four distinct composite figure types, ranging from dense multi-layer views combining a warped score with waveform, spectrogram, and chromagram, to lightweight waveform-and-beat displays, all generated through the same composition mechanism. RQ2 was examined through a qualitative comparison with Sonic Visualiser, Piano Precision, and manual SVG editing, showing that LayerIt is the only one of these workflows that keeps a continuously time-warped score, arbitrary algorithmic overlays, and re-runnable, script-based reproducibility together in the same figure.

    \item \textbf{Objective 3} was assessed through a case study built around beat-tracking output for BWV~856, in which RQ3 was addressed by demonstrating how a shared-axis visualisation supports the qualitative diagnosis of beat-tracking results and can serve as a reference for annotation. The same validation was equally honest about where this support breaks down, namely for highly expressive performances and sparse onset annotations, rather than presenting the framework as a universal solution.
\end{itemize}

Beyond the framework itself, this thesis produced concrete, reusable outputs: LayerIt as an open-source, pip-installable Python library; an open-source repository of the prototypes developed throughout the project; and a Late-Breaking Demo paper submitted to ISMIR 2026. Just as importantly, the iterative process described in \autoref{chap:Methodology} is itself an achievement of the work: by building and discarding five successive prototypes, the thesis identified that beat annotation and beat-tracking visualisation are, in practice, two separate problems, and it deliberately narrowed its scope to the one that offered the clearest and most feasible contribution. That decision is what allowed LayerIt to be evaluated against well-defined criteria rather than being spread thin across an interactive \ac{GUI} and a visualisation framework at once.

\section{Future Work}

The limitations identified in \autoref{chap:Validation} point directly to the most pressing follow-up work. First, the claims made about reduced manual effort (RQ2) and extensibility were assessed qualitatively; a controlled study is needed to quantify them, for instance by measuring the wall-clock time and number of manual operations required to reproduce a given figure with LayerIt versus Sonic Visualiser or manual SVG editing, and by implementing a genuinely new \texttt{Layer} subclass and reporting the code changes it requires. Second, the fidelity of LayerIt's visualisations is bounded by the quality of the underlying score-performance alignment; dedicated experiments comparing dense and sparse MAPS annotations, and stress-testing the framework against expressive performances with substantial rubato or structural deviations from the score, would clarify precisely where the time-warped representation remains reliable.

Beyond validating what already exists, several directions could extend LayerIt's scope. The framework currently integrates a single alignment method (ScoreWarp) and a single beat-tracking algorithm (BeatThis); supporting additional alignment and \ac{MIR} algorithms as further \texttt{Layer} implementations would test the extensibility claims made in this thesis under more demanding conditions and grow LayerIt into a more general-purpose visualisation toolkit. The case study in this thesis was also limited to a single solo piano excerpt; validating the framework against a broader range of instrumentations, genres, and performance styles would strengthen the generality of its conclusions. Finally, having deliberately set aside the interaction problem in \autoref{chap:Methodology}, a natural long-term direction is to revisit it: the SVG output produced by LayerIt already preserves the addressable structure inherited from Verovio and MEI, which could be leveraged to build an interactive, browser-based annotation layer on top of the visualisations this thesis already knows how to generate. Pursuing this direction would reconnect the visualisation contribution made here with the beat-annotation and beat-tracking-evaluation tools that originally motivated this thesis.

\section{Final Remarks}
This thesis began with the observation of a critical gap in the MIR research community: the absence of a unified framework for generating time-aligned, multi-modal visualisations suited to contemporary research workflows. Through an iterative development process and careful architectural design, we have delivered LayerIt, a Python-based, object-oriented tool that addresses this gap while explicitly supporting future extension and integration.

LayerIt’s value lies not merely in its immediate applicability to beat tracking and annotation tasks, but in its demonstration that visualisation frameworks can be architected for extensibility, composability, and community adoption. By grounding the system in established conventions (OO design patterns, common MIR libraries, standardized formats), we have created a foundation upon which the community can build up on. 

As MIR research continues to evolve, driven by advances in machine learning, increasing dataset complexity, and growing interdisciplinary collaboration. The need for sophisticated, flexible visualisation tools will only intensify. LayerIt is positioned to serve as a foundation for this future work, enabling researchers to focus on their core innovations rather than reimplementing visualisation infrastructure from scratch.
